A palace at Mycenae or Ugarit could grow its own grain, press its own oil, and weave its own cloth. What it could not do was make bronze. The tin came from somewhere east of Mesopotamia — perhaps Afghanistan — and the copper from Cyprus.

§5 · pp. 61–64

Kings as brothers

The letters exchanged between the great courts of the fourteenth century are, on their surface, documents of warmth. Rulers address one another as brothers. They ask after each other's households, horses, and chariots, and they send extravagant gifts.

But the language of kinship in these archives is a protocol, not a description. It regulates what may be asked for and in what tone; it does not tell us who needed whom. A king who must write three times for a shipment of copper is not, whatever he is called, an equal.

The Alashiya correspondence is the sharpest case. Cyprus held the copper, and the Cypriot king writes with a confidence that sits oddly beside the deferential formulas he is obliged to use. Historians have read this either as evidence of a genuine multipolar order or as evidence that the formulas had come loose from the realities they once described.

We should be candid that the archive is partial. What survives at Amarna is what one Egyptian administration chose to keep, in one place, for a few decades. Arguments about the whole system rest on a keyhole view of it.

Even so, the keyhole shows something unmistakable: an economy of prestige goods that moved by sea, in quantity, under royal sanction…
